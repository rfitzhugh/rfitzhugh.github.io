# The Org Chart Changes. Your Reputation Doesn't.

In five and a half years at Atlassian, I reported to roughly ten different heads of engineering. Not ten managers — ten heads of engineering, several levels up, the kind of change that cascades a new strategy, a new set of priorities, and often a new team structure down through everyone beneath it. Reorgs weren't an occasional disruption to my tenure there. They were the operating environment.

I don't say that to complain. I say it because it's the honest starting point for this post. A lot of advice about organizational change is written by people who've lived through one or two reorgs and are still processing the experience. This isn't that. This is what I've learned from treating reorg frequency as a constant rather than an anomaly, across two very different environments — a startup at Rubrik that grew from under 200 people to over 2,000 during my tenure, and an enterprise at Atlassian where the strategy above me shifted on what felt like an annual cadence even as the mission underneath it stayed remarkably stable.

The short version: reorgs reshape the social system far more than they reshape the technical one. The engineers who come out ahead aren't the ones who predict the next reorg or politically maneuver around it. They're the ones who've built something that doesn't depend on the current org chart to be true.

## Separate the strategy from the evaluation

The first thing a reorg does to people, psychologically, is make them ask the wrong question. "Why did this happen to me?" assumes the reorg was a verdict on their performance. Almost never is that the actual mechanism.

Reorgs are downstream of budget cycles, executive priorities, market conditions, acquisitions, and product bets that were made in rooms that had nothing to do with any individual engineer's output. When Rubrik moved from an appliance-based product to a SaaS-first model, that reshaped my org, my title, and my mandate — and it had exactly nothing to do with whether I was good at my job. It had to do with where the company needed to place its bets next.

The more useful question, and the one I've trained myself to ask first, is: given the new landscape, where can I have the most impact? That question is answerable. "Why did this happen to me" usually isn't, and chasing an answer to it mostly just burns time you could spend getting oriented in whatever comes next.

## Focus on what's actually yours to control

Reporting lines, executive decisions, headcount allocation, which teams get merged or split — none of that is yours to control, and treating it as though it should be is a fast way to spend your energy on things that don't move. What is yours: the depth of your technical growth, the relationships you've built, the quality of what you ship, how clearly you communicate, and how ready you are for whatever opportunity shows up next.

This isn't resignation. It's a filter. Every time I've watched someone burn out during a reorg cycle, it's because they kept re-litigating decisions several levels above their pay grade instead of redirecting that same energy into the parts of the situation they could actually shape. The people who come through reorgs well aren't the ones who feel less friction — they're the ones who've gotten disciplined about where they spend their attention.

## Get good at reducing uncertainty for other people

Distinguished engineers aren't usually distinguished by writing meaningfully better code than everyone around them. They're distinguished by their ability to create clarity when a situation doesn't have any yet — and a reorg is exactly the kind of situation that runs short on clarity. Ownership boundaries blur. Interfaces between teams that used to be obvious stop being obvious. Nobody's quite sure who owns what for a few weeks or months.

Part of why that gap opens up in the first place is [Conway's Law](https://rfitzhugh.github.io/posts/laws-i-keep-coming-back-to/#conways-law-your-architecture-is-an-organizational-fossil): systems end up shaped like the communication structures that built them. A reorg changes the communication structure overnight — new team boundaries, new reporting lines, new handoffs — but the architecture doesn't move that fast. For a while, the code and the org chart disagree with each other. The interface that used to map cleanly to a single team now spans two. The service one team owned end to end is suddenly split across a boundary nobody's formally drawn yet. That mismatch is the ambiguity, and it's temporary only if someone actively closes it.

That gap is an opportunity, not just a nuisance to wait out. During every reorg I've been part of, the engineers who came out of it with more scope, not less, were the ones who volunteered to write the doc nobody had gotten around to writing, to define the interface between two newly separated teams, to name the risk everyone was quietly worried about but hadn't said out loud. I've written elsewhere about the broader discipline of deciding well under genuine uncertainty — the short version here is that people who reduce it for others become disproportionately valuable, and a reorg is one of the highest-leverage moments to demonstrate that.

## Build a reputation that doesn't live inside the org chart

Most engineers know their immediate team well and almost nobody else. That's fine until the day those relationships disappear overnight, which is exactly what a reorg does. The people who weather that well are the ones who'd already spent time before the reorg building relationships with engineers on adjacent teams, with product and design partners, with engineering managers and staff-plus engineers outside their immediate reporting line.

Titles change. Managers change. Reporting chains get redrawn on a slide in a leadership offsite you weren't in. What doesn't change is whether the people around you already know what you're capable of. Across ten changes in engineering leadership, the throughline that kept me relevant to each new regime wasn't my title at the time — it was that the people making the new org's decisions already had a working understanding of what I built and how I operated, because I'd made sure that understanding existed before I needed it.

If you want a shorthand for what to actually be known for: ship reliably, make ambiguous problems clearer, help other engineers succeed, and be someone people trust when things are stressful. Every one of those is portable. None of them requires a specific team, manager, or org chart to remain true.

## Optimize for learning, not for team identity

It's easy to build your professional identity around a specific team or mission, and reorgs are very good at breaking that attachment without asking permission. The healthier anchor is a running answer to three questions: am I building valuable technical depth, am I growing my ability to influence work beyond my immediate scope, and am I expanding who I know and who knows me. Those three things keep compounding in value regardless of what your reporting line looks like next quarter. Team identity resets with every reorg. Skill, influence, and network don't.

## Stay adaptable without becoming directionless

There's a real difference between flexibility and chasing whatever the newest priority happens to be, and reorg-heavy environments make it easy to confuse the two. The balance I've found useful: support whatever the current business priority actually is, while continuing to invest in the durable skills underneath it — distributed systems, security, infrastructure, and increasingly, applied AI and the judgment to know where it actually helps. Business priorities move faster than foundational skills do. If you let your skill investment track the priority of the month, you end up rebuilding your foundation every time leadership changes, which is exhausting and slow. If your foundation is durable, you can redirect it toward whatever the new priority is in a fraction of the time.

## Document your impact before you need it

Don't wait for review season to reconstruct what you did. Keep a running record — projects shipped, problems solved, metrics moved, positive feedback, mentoring and technical leadership you provided — as you go. I've written a longer, more prescriptive version of how I do this elsewhere, but the reorg-specific reason it matters is simple: when your manager changes, and across ten changes in leadership it will, you don't want to be reconstructing six months of impact from memory for someone who's never seen your work before. You want to hand them a document that already exists.

## Invest in career capital outside the walls of your current org

Even when you're genuinely happy where you are, keep a résumé that's actually current, a network that extends beyond your immediate team, a portfolio of work you can speak to concretely, and enough recent interview practice that a conversation with another company doesn't feel like starting from zero. Stay aware of what the market looks like, even if you have no intention of using that awareness anytime soon.

The reason this matters isn't that you should expect to leave. It's that people who know they have options consistently handle organizational change with less stress than people who feel trapped by it — and that's true whether or not they ever actually exercise those options. Optionality is calming even when it's unused.

## The through-line

Ten changes in engineering leadership taught me that the org chart is one of the least stable things about a career, and one of the least important ones to optimize for. What's stable is the work you can point to, the people who've already seen you do it well, and the skills that keep compounding no matter who you report to next. Reorgs will keep happening. They always do. The engineers who do well through them aren't the ones who found a way to avoid the churn — they're the ones who built something the churn couldn't take from them.
